# The Worldview of Daniel Schmachtenberger

## A Comprehensive Breakdown

---

## Who He Is

Daniel Schmachtenberger is a social philosopher, systems thinker, and founding member of The Consilience Project. His work sits at the intersection of existential risk, civilizational design, game theory, complexity science, and ethics. He is not primarily an academic — he is a synthesizer, someone who pulls from ecology, economics, philosophy, cognitive science, information theory, and military strategy to construct a unified picture of the crisis facing humanity and what it would take to survive it.

His central project is both diagnostic and prescriptive: to identify the *deep structural reasons* why civilization appears to be heading toward self-termination, and to articulate the design constraints of any civilization that could avoid that fate.

---

## The Core Thesis: The Metacrisis

Schmachtenberger's foundational concept is the **metacrisis** — not any single crisis (climate change, AI risk, nuclear proliferation, biodiversity collapse, political polarization, information warfare), but the *underlying pattern that generates all of them simultaneously*.

His argument is not that these crises happen to coincide. It is that they share root causes — what he calls **generator functions** — and that any attempt to solve one crisis in isolation will either fail or displace the problem into another domain. You solve carbon emissions but accelerate resource extraction. You regulate AI in one country but create an arms race in another. You address economic inequality through growth but intensify ecological overshoot.

The metacrisis is therefore not a list of problems. It is a claim about the *architecture* of civilization itself: the structure of our coordination systems, incentive landscapes, and relationship to technology is such that it *inevitably produces* catastrophic and existential risk. The structure is the problem.

---

## The Two Generator Functions

Schmachtenberger identifies two deep generator functions that underlie virtually all human-caused existential risk:

### Generator Function 1: Rivalrous Games × Exponential Technology

The first and most prominent generator function is the combination of **win-lose competitive dynamics** with **exponentially increasing technological power**.

Rivalrous games — any situation where one party's gain comes at another's expense — have always produced harm. Wars, exploitation, resource hoarding, political manipulation. But for most of human history, the harm was bounded by the limited power of the technology available. A sword can only kill so many people. A lie can only travel so far.

The argument is that exponential technology removes that bound. Nuclear weapons, engineered pathogens, advanced AI, autonomous weapons systems, mass surveillance infrastructure — these are all the *same competitive dynamics humans have always engaged in*, but now amplified to civilizational or species-level consequence. The logic is simple and stark:

> Rivalrous dynamics have been the generator function of almost all the things humans have ever done that have harmed each other and the biosphere. When you multiply those dynamics by exponential technology, the harm becomes existential. And since exponential technology cannot be uninvented, we either figure out anti-rivalrous coordination or the human experiment completes.

This is not a moral argument. It is a structural one. The game theory of competition in a world of exponentially increasing technological capacity trends toward mutual destruction as a mathematical inevitability.

### Generator Function 2: Complicated Systems Subsuming Their Complex Substrate

The second generator function is subtler but equally important. It concerns the relationship between human-built systems and the natural world.

Schmachtenberger makes a sharp distinction between **complex systems** and **complicated systems**:

- **Complex systems** (ecosystems, the biosphere, evolved biological networks) are self-organizing, self-repairing, closed-loop, and antifragile. They recycle waste, maintain homeostasis, and increase in resilience through perturbation.

- **Complicated systems** (industrial infrastructure, supply chains, financial markets, engineered technology) are designed, fragile, open-loop, and dependent on external inputs. They produce waste that does not get recycled. They require continuous maintenance and energy input. They break in ways that cascade.

The crisis is that humanity's complicated systems are progressively *subsuming* — consuming, degrading, and replacing — the complex substrate (the biosphere) they depend on. Every factory, city, and supply chain draws resources from and deposits waste into the biosphere. The open-loop nature of our built world means we are simultaneously *depleting* the substrate (extracting faster than it regenerates) and *accumulating* waste in it (polluting faster than it can absorb).

As the biosphere's complexity decreases, its own antifragility degrades. Climate systems that were once self-stabilizing begin to enter positive feedback loops. Biodiversity loss passes tipping points beyond which ecosystem functions collapse. The very substrate that makes human civilization possible becomes too degraded to support it.

---

## The Underlying Problem: Choice Without Ethics

Beneath both generator functions, Schmachtenberger identifies a deeper philosophical gap: the mismatch between our **theory of causation** and our **theory of choice**.

Science has given humanity an extraordinarily powerful theory of causation. We understand physics, chemistry, biology, and engineering well enough to split atoms, edit genomes, and build artificial intelligence. This understanding translates directly into technological power — the ability to cause enormous effects in the world.

But we have no comparably rigorous theory of choice. We have no well-grounded, widely shared ethical framework that tells us *how to use* that power. Religion used to serve this function, but science rejected religious ethics along with religious cosmology. Philosophy has produced many competing ethical frameworks, none with the authority or universality of scientific knowledge.

In the absence of a real theory of choice, the *default* theory has been **game theory** — the mathematics of strategic competition. And game theory, in a world of exponential technology, leads directly to the two generator functions described above. The entire metacrisis, in this framing, is the result of having *godlike power* with *no ethical framework adequate to wielding it*.

---

## Multipolar Traps: The Mechanism of Civilizational Self-Destruction

The central mechanism through which the metacrisis operates is the **multipolar trap** — Schmachtenberger's generalization of concepts from game theory (the tragedy of the commons, arms races, prisoner's dilemmas, races to the bottom) into a single category.

A multipolar trap is any situation where:

1. **Multiple agents** (people, companies, nations) each face a choice.
2. **The locally optimal choice for each individual agent**, if pursued, leads to the **globally worst outcome for all agents**.
3. **No single agent can afford to unilaterally defect** from the locally optimal strategy, because doing so would mean being outcompeted, conquered, or destroyed by those who continue to pursue it.
4. **The system therefore locks all agents into a trajectory** that none of them individually want but none of them can individually escape.

The arms race is the canonical example. No nation wants a world bristling with nuclear weapons. But any nation that unilaterally disarms becomes vulnerable to those that don't. So everyone arms, and the world becomes more dangerous for all.

Schmachtenberger argues that this is not an edge case. It is the *dominant dynamic of civilization*. The global economy is a multipolar trap (compete or die, even if competition destroys the commons). The AI race is a multipolar trap (build fast or be outpaced, even if building fast is unsafe). Social media engagement is a multipolar trap (optimize for attention or lose relevance, even if attention optimization corrodes public discourse). Fishing, logging, mining, agriculture — all multipolar traps at global scale.

He draws on Scott Alexander's 2014 essay "Meditations on Moloch" to give this dynamic a mythological name: **Moloch** — the ancient deity to whom people sacrificed their children. Moloch is the *god of coordination failures*, the emergent force that compels rational agents to collectively produce outcomes none of them want. Schmachtenberger's contribution is to argue that Moloch is not a metaphor but a structural feature of any system organized around rivalrous incentives, and that exponential technology is turning Moloch from a source of suffering into a source of extinction.

---

## The Three Attractors

Given this analysis, Schmachtenberger frames civilization as gravitating toward one of **three attractors** — three basins of possibility that the dynamics of the present tend to pull us into:

### Attractor 1: Catastrophe

The unregulated continuation of current dynamics — rivalrous competition multiplied by exponentially increasing technology — leads to cascading catastrophes. Not necessarily a single dramatic extinction event, but an accelerating series of collapses: ecological systems passing tipping points, economic systems failing, regional conflicts escalating, pandemics (natural or engineered), AI-induced disruptions, and so on. Each catastrophe weakens the systems that might have prevented the next, producing a cascade.

### Attractor 2: Dystopia

The "solution" to catastrophe that current power structures are most likely to pursue is centralized authoritarian control — mass surveillance, social credit systems, algorithmic governance, concentration of power in the hands of whoever controls the most advanced technology. This prevents some catastrophes by constraining individual action, but at the cost of human freedom, agency, and self-determination. Schmachtenberger argues this is also unstable in the long run, because centralized systems cannot innovate fast enough to adapt to changing conditions (they lose to "Red Queen dynamics"), and because the concentration of power itself becomes an existential risk.

### Attractor 3: The Third Attractor

This is what Schmachtenberger is seeking. A civilizational configuration that avoids both catastrophe and dystopia. Not a utopia — he is careful about that word — but a system that is:

- **Anti-rivalrous**: structured so that agents' interests are aligned with the interests of the whole, making competition against the commons structurally impossible or irrational.
- **Antifragile**: able to absorb shocks and grow stronger from perturbation, rather than cascading into collapse.
- **Self-propagating**: able to maintain and reproduce its own conditions of existence without continuous external intervention.

The third attractor requires fundamentally new coordination mechanisms, new economic logics, new information ecologies, and new relationships to technology. It requires solving multipolar traps *as a category*, not one at a time.

---

## Anti-Rivalry: The Design Principle

At the center of Schmachtenberger's prescriptive vision is the concept of **anti-rivalry** — a condition beyond mere cooperation where *your gain is structurally my gain*.

He distinguishes:

- **Rivalrous goods**: Things whose consumption by one person reduces availability for others (food, oil, territory). Ownership of rivalrous goods puts people in structural competition.
- **Anti-rivalrous goods**: Things whose use by one person *increases* their value to everyone else (knowledge, open-source software, shared infrastructure). In an anti-rivalrous system, participating enriches the commons rather than depleting it.

The transition from rivalrous to anti-rivalrous systems is, in his view, the core design challenge of our time. One concrete illustration he gives: moving from private ownership of cars (a rivalrous good — your car sitting in a parking lot is resource removed from circulation) to shared autonomous transportation (an anti-rivalrous service — everyone has better access, fewer resources consumed, and each person's use of the system generates data and infrastructure improvements that benefit all others).

The deeper point is that in a world of rivalrous goods and ownership dynamics, *every agent is structurally incentivized to hoard and compete*. In a world of anti-rivalrous commonwealth access, the incentive structure inverts: enriching the commons is the rational thing to do.

## Sensemaking: The Information Ecology Problem

A major thread of Schmachtenberger's work concerns the collapse of shared sensemaking — humanity's collective ability to accurately perceive reality and make decisions based on that perception.

His argument is that in a game-theoretic environment where information equals competitive advantage, **disinformation becomes a dominant strategy**. Everyone has an incentive to manipulate the information environment — corporations to sell products, politicians to gain power, nations to undermine rivals, individuals to gain status. The result is an information ecosystem in which:

- Signal is drowned in noise.
- Reliable information is indistinguishable from manufactured narratives.
- Every institution that was supposed to curate reliable information (journalism, science, government) has been captured by the same competitive dynamics that corrupt everything else.
- People lose the ability to form shared models of reality, making collective action (which is the only way to solve global coordination problems) essentially impossible.

He considers the destruction of shared sensemaking to be *possibly the nearest-term existential risk*, because without the ability to accurately perceive problems, no other problem can be addressed. This is the motivation behind the **Consilience Project**, which aims not to produce more information but to improve the *processes* by which people make sense of information — to upgrade sensemaking infrastructure at a civilizational level.

---

## The Relationship Between Power and Strength

Schmachtenberger draws an important distinction between **power** and **strength**:

- **Power** is the ability to impose your will on others or the environment. Power operates through domination, coercion, and extraction. It is inherently rivalrous — your power is often at the expense of others' autonomy.

- **Strength** is the ability to maintain sovereignty, integrity, and coherence in the presence of outside forces. Strength is not about beating others. It is about being un-corruptible, un-cooptable, and self-determined.

He argues that civilization has optimized for *power* — the capacity to control and extract — while neglecting *strength* — the capacity for individuals and systems to be self-governing, resilient, and aligned with their own values. A civilization of powerful but weak agents is one that cannot resist multipolar traps, because agents without strength are easily captured by perverse incentives.

The development of individual and collective *strength* — what you might call integrity, sovereignty, or coherent agency — is in his view a prerequisite for any functional post-crisis civilization.

---

## Why Incentives Are Intrinsically Problematic

One of Schmachtenberger's more radical claims is that **incentives themselves are a problem**, not just perverse incentives.

The standard view in economics and governance is that the way to produce good outcomes is to align incentives — make it profitable to do good things and costly to do bad things. Schmachtenberger argues that this approach is fundamentally flawed because:

1. Any incentive system can be gamed. If you reward X, people will find ways to produce the appearance of X without the substance.
2. Incentives co-opt sovereignty. When someone acts because of an incentive rather than because they understand and care about the outcome, their agency has been captured. They are no longer choosing — they are responding to a stimulus.
3. Replacing perverse incentives with "positive" incentives does not solve the structural problem. It just changes what people are being manipulated into doing.

His alternative is a system where people *understand the consequences of their actions* well enough to make good choices without external incentives — where the feedback loops between action and consequence are transparent, direct, and comprehensible. This requires both a radical upgrade in collective intelligence and information infrastructure, and a culture that cultivates genuine care for the commons rather than compliance with incentive structures.

## Complex vs. Complicated: A Civilizational Design Framework

Schmachtenberger's distinction between complex and complicated systems generates a set of design constraints for any viable civilization:

### Closed-Loop Systems
Human systems must become closed-loop, like ecosystems. Every output must become an input somewhere else. Waste must be eliminated as a category — not merely reduced, but designed out of existence. This is not just an environmental claim; it is a systems-architecture claim about the only kind of civilization that can persist indefinitely.

### Right Relationship Between Complex and Complicated
Complicated (human-built) systems must exist in a relationship with complex (natural) systems that supports rather than degrades the complex system's capacity for self-organization and regeneration. Currently, the relationship is parasitic — complicated systems consume complex systems. It must become symbiotic.

### Anti-Fragility
Systems must be designed so that perturbation makes them stronger, not weaker. This is Nassim Taleb's concept applied at civilizational scale. A civilization that becomes more fragile as it grows more powerful is on a collision course with reality.

## On Consciousness, Cognition, and the Inner Dimension

While much of Schmachtenberger's work is structural and systemic, he is equally insistent that the metacrisis has an **inner dimension** — a psychological and even spiritual component.

He has engaged extensively with thinkers like **Iain McGilchrist** (on the divided brain and the dominance of left-hemisphere, reductive, mechanistic cognition over right-hemisphere, relational, holistic cognition) and **John Vervaeke** (on the meaning crisis and the loss of participatory knowing). He has also referenced **David Bohm's** concept of the implicate order — the idea that consciousness and reality are more fundamentally interconnected than materialist science assumes.

His position on consciousness is notable: he rejects both strong computationalism (the view that consciousness is just computation, and therefore AI will naturally become conscious) and simple emergent physicalism (the view that consciousness just "emerges" from enough physical complexity). He believes self-organization and embodiment are essential to consciousness, and that the nature of consciousness is relevant to the metacrisis because:

1. **The way we cognize shapes the world we build.** A civilization built by minds that fragment, reduce, and abstract reality into controllable pieces will build fragmented, reductive, and fragile systems. Addressing the metacrisis requires a transformation in how humans *think* and *perceive*, not just what institutions they build.

2. **The meaning crisis is part of the metacrisis.** People who lack a sense of meaning, connection, and purpose are more vulnerable to manipulation, addiction, tribalism, and nihilism — all of which feed the dynamics that generate existential risk.

3. **Ethical development requires psychological development.** The capacity to hold complexity, tolerate uncertainty, take multiple perspectives, and care about distant consequences is not automatic. It requires cultivation — a kind of practice that modern civilization has largely abandoned.

## On Artificial Intelligence

Schmachtenberger's view of AI risk is distinctive because he embeds it within the metacrisis framework rather than treating it as a standalone problem.

His key arguments:

1. **AI accelerates the metacrisis.** Even without achieving superintelligence, current AI systems accelerate every dynamic that produces existential risk — they intensify information warfare, automate economic competition, enable mass surveillance, lower the barrier to engineered pathogens, and make weapons systems faster and more autonomous. AI does not have to be misaligned to be dangerous; it is dangerous because it amplifies an already misaligned civilization.

2. **You cannot build aligned AI in a misaligned context.** The AI alignment problem is not a narrow technical challenge. It is embedded in a civilization whose coordination mechanisms, incentive structures, and information ecologies are themselves misaligned with long-term human wellbeing. An AI built by corporations competing for market share, funded by investors seeking returns, and deployed in an attention economy optimized for engagement will inevitably reflect and amplify those misaligned priorities. Solving AI alignment requires first solving civilizational alignment.

3. **AI safety is a multipolar trap.** Any company or nation that pauses AI development to ensure safety risks being outpaced by competitors who do not pause. This is a textbook multipolar trap, and no amount of safety research within a single organization can solve it because the problem is structural.

4. **AI makes other risks more complex.** AI increases the complexity of the risk landscape itself — creating new categories of risk, making existing risks harder to monitor and predict, and producing systems whose behavior is opaque even to their creators (the "black box" problem). This compounds the sensemaking crisis.

## The Superorganism Lens

A recurring metaphor in Schmachtenberger's work — shared with his collaborator Nate Hagens — is the idea of humanity as a **metabolic superorganism**: a planetary-scale system that consumes energy and resources, produces waste, and grows according to its own internal logic, often at the expense of its host system (the biosphere).

This is not a claim that humanity is literally a biological organism. It is an analytical lens: if you zoom out far enough and look at humanity's aggregate behavior — energy consumption, resource extraction, waste production, information processing — it looks like a single organism that is eating its environment faster than the environment can regenerate. The "superorganism" is not conscious or intentional; it is an emergent property of billions of individual agents following local incentives. But its behavior is predictable and its trajectory is unsustainable.

The superorganism lens is useful because it reframes the metacrisis as a problem of *metabolism* rather than morality. The question is not "why are humans bad?" but "why does the aggregate system behave in self-destructive ways despite being composed of agents who individually do not want self-destruction?" The answer, again, is structural: the coordination mechanisms that connect individual behavior to collective outcomes are broken.

## How He Thinks: The Schmachtenberger Method

Beyond specific ideas, Schmachtenberger has a distinctive *mode* of thinking that is worth understanding:

### Generator Function Analysis
Rather than addressing symptoms, he always asks: what is the *generator function* — the underlying dynamic that produces this class of problems? If you can identify and address the generator function, you solve all the symptoms simultaneously. If you address only symptoms, you are playing whack-a-mole with an exponentially accelerating system.

### Holding Multiple Problems Simultaneously
He insists that viable solutions must address *all* major risks simultaneously. A solution that reduces one risk while increasing another is not a solution — it is a displacement. This is why his thinking tends to be so comprehensive and interconnected; he refuses to let problems be considered in isolation because they do not exist in isolation.

### Design Constraint Thinking
Rather than proposing specific policies or technologies, he articulates *design constraints* that any viable civilization must satisfy. These constraints function as a filter: any proposed solution can be evaluated against them. Does it increase anti-rivalry? Does it close loops? Does it degrade or support the complex substrate? Does it create new multipolar traps?

### First-Principles Reconstruction
He treats no existing institution or system as sacred. Markets, democracy, nation-states, property rights — all are evaluated from first principles against the question: does this contribute to or detract from civilizational viability? This makes his thinking uncomfortable to people attached to any existing system, because he is willing to argue that *all* existing systems need fundamental redesign.

### Synthesis Across Domains
He draws freely from ecology, game theory, complexity science, philosophy of mind, political economy, military strategy, information theory, and developmental psychology. His distinctive contribution is not in any one domain but in the *connections* between domains — in showing how arms-race dynamics in geopolitics, attention-economy dynamics in social media, metabolic dynamics in the global economy, and cognitive dynamics in individual human minds are all expressions of the same underlying patterns.

## The Path Forward (As He Sees It)

Schmachtenberger is careful not to be utopian. He does not claim to have a blueprint for the third attractor. But he identifies several *necessary conditions*:

1. **Solve multipolar traps as a category.** Not one at a time, but categorically. This requires coordination mechanisms that do not yet exist — systems that make defection against the commons structurally irrational, not just morally discouraged.

2. **Transition from rivalrous to anti-rivalrous systems.** Move from ownership of scarce goods to shared access to commonwealth resources. Design economic systems where enriching the commons is the rational strategy.

3. **Close the loops.** Redesign human systems to be closed-loop — no waste, no depletion, full recycling of all outputs into inputs. Align complicated human systems with the complex natural systems they depend on.

4. **Rebuild sensemaking.** Create information ecologies where accurate perception of reality is possible. This is not about "fighting misinformation" — it is about building new epistemic infrastructure that is structurally resistant to manipulation.

5. **Develop a real theory of choice.** Create an ethical and philosophical framework adequate to the power of exponential technology. This must be as rigorous and universally compelling as science's theory of causation.

6. **Cultivate individual strength and sovereignty.** Develop education and culture that produces people capable of holding complexity, resisting manipulation, thinking long-term, and caring about consequences beyond their immediate self-interest.

7. **Build collective intelligence that scales.** Create systems of group decision-making that become *more* intelligent and effective as they include more people, rather than less. Current systems (democracy, markets, bureaucracies) all degrade in quality as they scale. The third attractor requires the opposite.

## The Emotional and Existential Dimension

One of the less discussed but important aspects of Schmachtenberger's work is his honesty about the *emotional experience* of understanding the metacrisis.

He acknowledges that genuinely onboarding this analysis — really understanding the scope and depth of the risks, the inadequacy of current approaches, and the difficulty of the required transformation — produces overwhelming despair. He has spoken with Nate Hagens about the psychological difficulty of holding this awareness, the pull toward false certainty (either optimistic or pessimistic), and the challenge of continuing to do meaningful work in the face of such uncertainty.

His resolution is not to minimize the difficulty but to reframe it: the work of addressing the metacrisis is not separate from the work of individual psychological development, sovereignty, and even spiritual growth. Becoming the kind of person who can hold complexity without collapsing into despair, cynicism, or grandiosity is itself part of the solution. The inner transformation and the outer transformation are the same project.

## Summary: The Schmachtenberger Worldview in Ten Statements

1. Civilization is heading toward self-termination, not because of any single crisis but because of the *structure* of our coordination systems.

2. All human-caused existential risks share common generator functions: rivalrous dynamics multiplied by exponential technology, and complicated systems consuming their complex substrate.

3. These generator functions produce multipolar traps — situations where rational individual action leads to collective catastrophe — which are the dominant dynamic of current civilization.

4. The two most likely outcomes (attractors) are cascading catastrophe or authoritarian dystopia. Both are unacceptable and both are unstable.

5. A viable third attractor requires anti-rivalrous coordination, closed-loop systems, antifragility, and a civilizational upgrade in sensemaking and ethical reasoning.

6. AI is not a standalone risk but an accelerant of the metacrisis — it amplifies every existing misalignment and cannot be aligned within a misaligned civilization.

7. We have a powerful theory of causation (science) but no comparably rigorous theory of choice (ethics). This gap is the deepest root of the crisis.

8. The information ecology is so degraded that shared sensemaking — the prerequisite for collective action — is itself in existential danger.

9. Incentive structures are part of the problem, not the solution. The goal is a civilization where people act well because they understand consequences, not because they are bribed or threatened.

10. The inner dimension matters. The metacrisis cannot be solved by systems redesign alone — it requires a transformation in how humans think, perceive, relate, and find meaning.
