# The Mind of Richard Feynman

## I. The Central Conviction: Understanding Over Knowledge

If there is a single thread that runs through everything Feynman did, said, and believed, it is this: *knowing the name of something is not the same as understanding it.* He inherited this insight from his father, Melville Feynman, who taught him as a child to look at a bird and ask not "What is it called?" but "What is it doing? Why is it doing that?" The name, his father explained, tells you nothing about the bird. It tells you something about people.

This distinction — between labels and comprehension — became the organizing principle of Feynman's intellectual life. He distrusted formalism for its own sake. He resisted abstraction that could not be cashed out in concrete physical intuition. He measured his own understanding by a ruthless standard: *Can I explain this to a freshman? If not, I don't really understand it.*

This was not anti-intellectualism. Feynman was one of the most technically sophisticated physicists who ever lived. But he believed that genuine understanding always bottoms out in something simple, visual, and almost physical — a picture you can hold in your mind, a story you can walk through step by step. When understanding is real, it feels like something. When it is fake, it feels like memorization.


## II. Epistemology: What It Means to Know

### The Primacy of Doubt

Feynman regarded doubt not as a weakness but as the engine of science. He argued that the entire enterprise of scientific inquiry depends on a willingness to say "I don't know" — and to mean it, to sit with it, to let it motivate the next question rather than grasping for a premature answer.

He made this explicit in his 1955 address to the National Academy of Sciences and again in his famous 1966 address to the National Science Teachers' Association. Science, he said, is not a body of knowledge. It is a method of *not fooling yourself* — and you are the easiest person to fool.

This is a deeper claim than it appears. Feynman was not simply advocating skepticism. He was arguing that the psychological posture of uncertainty is *productive*. When you hold your beliefs lightly, you see things that committed believers miss. You notice the anomaly. You follow the thread that doesn't fit. The history of physics, he observed, is a history of people noticing that something was slightly wrong with the prevailing story.

### The Hierarchy of Evidence

Feynman had a clear hierarchy for evaluating claims:

**Direct experimental evidence** sits at the top. If nature says one thing and your theory says another, nature wins. Always. No exceptions. The elegance of a theory, the authority of its author, the consensus of the community — none of it matters if the experiment disagrees. He captured this in one of his most quoted lines: "It doesn't matter how beautiful your theory is, it doesn't matter how smart you are. If it disagrees with experiment, it's wrong."

**Logical consistency** comes next. A theory must not contradict itself. But logical consistency alone proves nothing about the physical world — it only tells you a theory *could* be true, not that it *is* true.

**Authority, tradition, and consensus** sit at the bottom. Feynman had an almost allergic reaction to arguments from authority. He did not care who said something. He cared whether it was right. This extended to his own work: he was willing to abandon ideas he had spent years developing if the evidence turned against them.

### The Problem with Philosophy

Feynman was famously dismissive of philosophy of science, but his actual position was more nuanced than the dismissal suggests. What he objected to was not philosophical thinking per se — he engaged in it constantly — but a particular style of philosophy that he felt substituted word-games for contact with reality. He thought philosophers of science spent too much time categorizing the *structure* of scientific theories and too little time doing the work of science itself. His concern was that meta-level reasoning, pursued in isolation from actual problem-solving, drifts into scholasticism.

At the same time, Feynman's own practice was deeply philosophical. His insistence on doubt, his analysis of what constitutes understanding, his careful distinctions between types of knowledge — these are philosophical moves. He was doing philosophy of science; he just refused to call it that.


## III. Method: How Feynman Thought Through Problems

### Multiple Representations

Perhaps Feynman's most distinctive cognitive habit was his insistence on approaching every problem from *multiple independent angles*. He did not trust any single formalism. When he learned a new result, he would re-derive it using a completely different method. When he encountered a puzzle, he would translate it into several different languages — algebraic, geometric, physical, computational — and check whether they all gave the same answer.

This was not redundancy for its own sake. Feynman discovered that each representation reveals features the others hide. An algebraic formulation might make symmetries visible. A geometric picture might make the physical mechanism intuitive. A computational approach might expose boundary cases that formal proofs glide over. By maintaining several representations simultaneously, Feynman could triangulate on the truth.

He described his own thinking process as heavily visual and kinesthetic. He thought in pictures, in physical analogies, in imagined mechanisms. When he developed the path integral formulation of quantum mechanics, he was quite literally imagining a particle exploring every possible path through space and time. The mathematics came afterward, as a way of making the picture precise.

### The Feynman Algorithm

His colleague Murray Gell-Mann once sarcastically described the "Feynman Algorithm" as: (1) Write down the problem. (2) Think very hard. (3) Write down the answer. This was meant as a joke about Feynman's apparent ability to solve problems by sheer force of intellect, but it actually captures something real about his approach.

Feynman did not follow systematic methodologies. He did not work through problems in linear, step-by-step fashion. Instead, he would immerse himself in a problem — often carrying it around for weeks or months, thinking about it in the shower, while driving, while eating — until a solution crystallized. He kept a mental roster of unsolved problems and constantly tested new techniques or ideas against them. When a new tool or insight appeared in any field, he would immediately ask: "Does this solve any of my problems?"

This meant that when breakthroughs came, they often appeared sudden to outside observers, as if Feynman had simply intuited the answer. In reality, the preparation had been going on for years. What looked like genius was the collision of deep preparation with the right moment.

### Simplification as Discovery

Feynman believed that if you cannot simplify an explanation, you do not yet understand the phenomenon. But he went further: he treated the *act of simplification itself* as a form of discovery. Stripping away unnecessary complexity often reveals the actual mechanism at work. Many of his most important contributions — Feynman diagrams, the path integral formulation, his work on superfluidity — were essentially acts of radical simplification. They did not add new facts to physics. They made existing facts *legible*.

This is related to his teaching philosophy. Feynman's legendary Caltech lectures were not simplified versions of real physics. They were his attempt to find the *simplest true account* of each phenomenon. The constraint of clarity forced him to think more deeply, not less. Several ideas that appeared first in the Feynman Lectures were genuinely novel — insights that emerged specifically because he was trying to explain things from first principles.

### First Principles Reasoning

Feynman was a relentless first-principles thinker. He distrusted inherited frameworks, standard derivations, and conventional wisdom — not because they were always wrong, but because accepting them without re-derivation meant inheriting their hidden assumptions. He wanted to know *why* each step was true, not just *that* it was true.

This practice had concrete consequences. Because Feynman re-derived everything from scratch, he often arrived at formulations that looked nothing like the standard ones. His version of quantum electrodynamics was barely recognizable to physicists trained in the conventional approach. It was the same theory, making the same predictions, but the machinery was completely different — and in many cases, far more efficient and revealing.

### Playing with Problems

Feynman insisted that the best work comes from play, not from grim determination. After his first wife Arline died and he felt burned out from the Manhattan Project, he made a deliberate decision to stop working on "important" problems and instead follow whatever caught his interest. He started analyzing the wobble of a spinning plate in the Cornell cafeteria — purely for fun, with no expectation that it would lead anywhere. The calculation led him back into quantum electrodynamics, which led to his Nobel Prize-winning work.

He drew a general lesson from this: the pressure to do "important" work is counterproductive. When you play with problems because they fascinate you, you follow unexpected connections. When you work on problems because they are prestigious, you follow well-worn paths. The history of science, Feynman argued, shows that the biggest discoveries come from people following their curiosity, not from people executing strategic research plans.


## IV. The Major Contributions

### Quantum Electrodynamics (QED)

Feynman's reformulation of quantum electrodynamics — the theory of how light and matter interact — is his most celebrated scientific achievement, for which he shared the 1965 Nobel Prize with Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga. All three had independently solved the same problem: how to make the theory give finite, physically meaningful answers rather than absurd infinities. But their approaches were starkly different, and Feynman's was the most radical.

The core problem was renormalization. In the original formulation of QED, calculations of even simple processes (like an electron scattering off a photon) produced infinite results. These infinities arose because the theory required summing over contributions from virtual particles at every energy scale, including arbitrarily high energies where the theory clearly could not be trusted.

Schwinger and Tomonaga solved this using a systematic, formal procedure — technically rigorous but computationally unwieldy. Feynman solved it by reimagining the entire theory from the ground up. He introduced a new way of thinking about particle interactions: instead of writing down equations of motion and solving them, he imagined every possible way a process could occur, assigned each possibility an amplitude (a complex number encoding both magnitude and phase), and summed them all up. This was the path integral approach to quantum mechanics, extended to quantum field theory.

Out of this came Feynman diagrams — simple pictures showing particles as lines and interactions as vertices. These diagrams were not merely pedagogical aids. Each diagram corresponded to a precise mathematical expression. Complex calculations that had taken Schwinger pages of dense algebra could be read off from a few diagrams. Feynman diagrams became the universal language of particle physics. To this day, every calculation in quantum field theory begins with drawing Feynman diagrams.

What made this contribution deep, not just convenient, was that it changed how physicists *think* about quantum processes. Before Feynman, quantum mechanics was understood primarily through the Schrödinger equation — a wave equation describing how quantum states evolve in time. Feynman's path integral formulation provided a completely different picture: a particle gets from A to B by taking *every possible path simultaneously*, with the probability of the outcome determined by the interference of all these paths. Both formulations give identical predictions, but the path integral picture makes certain features of quantum mechanics — especially symmetries and the connection to classical mechanics — far more transparent.

### The Path Integral Formulation of Quantum Mechanics

The path integral approach deserves separate treatment because its implications extend far beyond QED. Feynman's doctoral thesis, supervised by John Archibald Wheeler, laid the foundation.

The central idea: to calculate the probability that a quantum system goes from state A to state B, you do not solve a differential equation. Instead, you consider *every possible history* of the system — every path it could take through configuration space, including wild, physically absurd paths — and you assign each path a phase proportional to the classical action along that path. The total amplitude is the sum (integral) over all paths, and the probability is the absolute square of this amplitude.

In the classical limit, this formalism recovers ordinary Newtonian mechanics. The vast majority of paths cancel each other out through destructive interference; only the paths near the classical trajectory survive. The classical path is the one where the action is *stationary* — exactly the principle of least action from classical mechanics. Feynman had shown that the principle of least action, far from being a mysterious teleological principle, is simply what happens when you take the quantum sum-over-paths seriously and look at the large-scale limit.

The path integral formulation opened up entire fields of theoretical physics. It is the natural language for quantum field theory, statistical mechanics, and string theory. It provides the cleanest derivation of the connection between quantum mechanics and statistical mechanics (via Wick rotation). It is the foundation of lattice gauge theory, which is how physicists today compute the properties of the strong nuclear force.

### Superfluidity

Feynman's work on liquid helium in the 1950s showed his ability to penetrate a problem that seemed intractable. Below about 2.17 Kelvin, helium-4 becomes a superfluid — it flows without viscosity, climbs up the walls of containers, and exhibits other bizarre quantum behaviors at macroscopic scales.

Feynman applied his path integral methods to this problem and showed that superfluidity could be understood as a consequence of Bose-Einstein statistics applied to many interacting helium atoms. He developed a microscopic theory that explained the observed excitation spectrum (the "roton" minimum in the energy-momentum relation that Landau had postulated phenomenologically). He showed how quantized vortices arise naturally from the quantum-mechanical requirement that the superfluid wave function be single-valued.

This work demonstrated something important about Feynman's approach: he was not a narrow specialist. He moved freely between particle physics, condensed matter physics, and statistical mechanics, applying the same set of tools and the same style of thinking to very different systems.

### The Theory of Weak Interactions

In 1958, Feynman and Murray Gell-Mann proposed the V-A (vector minus axial vector) theory of the weak nuclear force. This was a critical step in understanding radioactive decay and the weak interaction.

The key insight was that the weak force violates parity (mirror symmetry) — a fact that had been experimentally demonstrated by C.S. Wu's famous experiment in 1957. Feynman and Gell-Mann showed that the mathematical structure of the weak interaction takes a specific form (V-A) that naturally incorporates maximal parity violation. This work was a precursor to the electroweak theory developed by Sheldon Glashow, Abdus Salam, and Steven Weinberg, which unified the weak and electromagnetic forces.

### Partons and the Strong Force

In the late 1960s, experiments at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center (SLAC) revealed that protons were not smooth, featureless objects. When hit with high-energy electrons, they scattered in ways that suggested the protons contained hard, point-like constituents.

Feynman proposed a model in which the proton is composed of "partons" — point-like particles that share the proton's momentum. His parton model provided a framework for interpreting the SLAC data and other deep inelastic scattering experiments. The partons were soon identified with the quarks that Gell-Mann had proposed on theoretical grounds. Feynman's parton model became a standard tool for analyzing high-energy collisions and remains central to how experiments at facilities like the Large Hadron Collider are interpreted today.

### Quantum Computing

In 1981, Feynman gave a keynote address at a conference on physics and computation in which he argued that classical computers are fundamentally incapable of efficiently simulating quantum systems. The number of variables needed to describe a quantum system grows exponentially with the system's size, making brute-force classical simulation intractable.

Feynman proposed the solution: use a *quantum* computer — a machine whose internal workings are themselves quantum-mechanical — to perform the simulation. This talk is widely regarded as the conceptual birth of quantum computing. It planted the seed that grew into the work of David Deutsch, Peter Shor, and the entire field of quantum information science.

### Nanotechnology

Feynman's 1959 lecture "There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom" is often cited as the founding document of nanotechnology. In it, he pointed out that the laws of physics impose no fundamental barrier to manipulating matter at the atomic scale. He envisioned machines that build smaller machines, which build still smaller machines, all the way down to devices that could arrange individual atoms.

He proposed specific challenges (writing the Encyclopedia Britannica on the head of a pin, building a working electric motor smaller than 1/64th of an inch) and offered cash prizes for achieving them. Both prizes were eventually claimed. The lecture anticipated developments in scanning tunneling microscopy, molecular engineering, and MEMS technology by decades.

### The Challenger Investigation

In 1986, Feynman served on the Rogers Commission investigating the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster. His role exemplified his approach to problem-solving: he bypassed bureaucratic channels, talked directly to engineers, and conducted his own experiments.

His most famous moment was the ice water demonstration during a televised hearing. He placed a sample of the O-ring rubber in a glass of ice water, removed it, and showed that it lost its resilience at low temperatures. This simple experiment cut through months of obfuscation and demonstrated in seconds what the engineers had been trying to communicate: the O-rings could not seal properly in cold weather, and the launch had proceeded on a cold morning over the objections of engineers.

His appendix to the Commission report — which NASA tried to suppress — is a masterpiece of clear thinking about institutional failure. He documented how NASA's management had gradually accepted increasing risk through a process of normalization of deviance. He noted the enormous gap between engineering estimates of shuttle reliability and management's claims. His concluding sentence became famous: "For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public relations, for Nature cannot be fooled."


## V. The Philosophy of Science

### Cargo Cult Science

Feynman's 1974 Caltech commencement address, "Cargo Cult Science," is his most sustained meditation on scientific integrity. The title refers to the cargo cults of Melanesia, where Pacific Islanders who had seen military supply planes during World War II built replica runways and control towers out of bamboo, hoping to attract the planes back. They had the *form* of an airport without the *substance*.

Feynman argued that much of what passes for science — in social science, education research, advertising, and even some corners of the natural sciences — is cargo cult science. It has the trappings of science (data collection, statistical analysis, peer review, jargon) but lacks the essential element: a ruthless commitment to not fooling yourself.

The key demand of scientific integrity, Feynman argued, is that you must report everything that might make your result unreliable. You must actively try to disprove your own hypothesis. You must describe your methods clearly enough that others can replicate your work. You must acknowledge the limitations of your conclusions. This is the opposite of advocacy, lawyering, or salesmanship — activities where you present the strongest possible case for your position and suppress unfavorable evidence.

He gave specific examples: researchers who designed experiments to confirm their theories and then explained away contradictory results. Educational programs that were implemented without proper controls and then declared successful based on anecdotes. Parapsychology research that kept finding effects just barely above statistical significance.

The deepest point in the address is about *self-deception*. Feynman argued that fooling yourself is the easiest thing in the world because you have the most motivation to do it. The safeguards of science — controlled experiments, peer review, replication — exist not because scientists are especially honest people, but because they are *ordinary* people who need institutional protection against their own biases.

### The Relation Between Science and Religion

Feynman addressed this topic directly in several lectures, most notably his 1956 talk "The Relation of Science and Religion." His position was nuanced and often mischaracterized.

He was an atheist, or at least an agnostic, but he did not argue that science disproves religion. His claim was narrower: science has demonstrated that it is possible to live with doubt, with uncertainty, with not knowing — and that this is a *valuable* way to live. Religion, in his view, often demands certainty where none is warranted. But he acknowledged that religion addresses questions (about meaning, ethics, purpose) that science does not, and he did not claim that science could replace those functions.

His most interesting argument was about *doubt as a moral and civic virtue*. He pointed out that most of the horrors of history were committed by people who were absolutely certain they were right. The ability to say "I'm not sure" — to hold your beliefs provisionally — is not just epistemically virtuous. It is ethically protective. It is the foundation of tolerance, open inquiry, and democratic self-governance.

### The Value of Science

In his essay "The Value of Science" (1955), Feynman identified three categories of value:

The first is **the power to do things** — the practical applications of scientific knowledge. He noted that this power is morally neutral: science gives us the ability to cure diseases and to build weapons, to feed populations and to poison environments. The power itself does not dictate how it is used.

The second is **intellectual enjoyment** — the sheer pleasure of understanding how the world works. Feynman was unapologetic about this. He thought the experience of understanding a deep scientific truth — seeing for the first time why the sky is blue, or how the heart pumps blood, or why the planets move in ellipses — is one of the great joys available to human beings, and that a culture that does not cultivate this joy is impoverished.

The third, and the one he considered most important, is **the habit of doubt and uncertainty**. Science teaches you to hold your beliefs provisionally, to test them against evidence, to revise them when the evidence changes. This habit of mind, Feynman argued, is science's most important contribution to civilization — more important than any specific technology or discovery.


## VI. On Education

Feynman's views on education were formed early and held consistently throughout his life. He visited Brazil in the 1950s to teach physics and was horrified by what he found: students who could recite definitions, reproduce derivations, and pass exams, but could not answer simple questions about the physical world. They had memorized physics without learning physics.

He diagnosed the problem as systematic: the educational system rewarded memorization and punished questioning. Students were not trained to ask "Does this make sense?" or "Can I think of an example?" They were trained to ask "Will this be on the exam?" The result was what he called "brittle" knowledge — knowledge that looked solid in the context of a test but shattered on contact with a real problem.

His prescription was simple in principle and difficult in practice: teach students to think, not to memorize. Start with phenomena, not formulas. Let students struggle with problems before giving them the tools to solve them. Test understanding by asking students to apply concepts in unfamiliar contexts, not to reproduce familiar derivations.

The Feynman Lectures on Physics (1961–1963) were his attempt to implement this philosophy. They remain, sixty years later, widely considered the best introductory physics textbook ever written — although paradoxically, they were too difficult for many of the Caltech freshmen they were originally designed for. The lectures work best for people who already know some physics and want to *understand* it for the first time.


## VII. Aesthetics and the Experience of Nature

Feynman had a deep aesthetic response to science, and he pushed back against the romantic notion that scientific understanding diminishes the beauty of nature. In a famous passage, he described looking at a flower with an artist friend. The friend argued that as an artist, he could appreciate the flower's beauty in a way that a scientist, who would "take it all apart," could not.

Feynman's response: the beauty the artist sees is available to the scientist too. But the scientist sees additional beauty — the intricate cellular structure, the evolutionary pressures that shaped the flower's color and form, the biochemical machinery of photosynthesis, the ecological relationships with pollinators. Science does not subtract from beauty. It adds layers of beauty that are invisible to the naked eye.

This was not a throwaway remark. It reflected a deep conviction that the universe as revealed by science is more wonderful, more strange, more beautiful than any pre-scientific account. The scale of the cosmos, the age of the Earth, the bizarre rules of quantum mechanics, the fact that stars are nuclear furnaces and that the atoms in your body were forged in those furnaces — these facts, Feynman insisted, should fill you with awe, not diminish it.

He expressed a similar idea about mathematics. He described the pleasure of discovering a mathematical result as being like the pleasure of discovering a beautiful landscape — you feel you have seen something that was there all along, waiting to be found.


## VIII. Character and Values

### Intellectual Honesty Above All

The value Feynman returned to most consistently was intellectual honesty — what he called "a kind of utter honesty, a kind of leaning over backwards" to avoid fooling yourself or others. This was not just a professional standard. It was, for Feynman, something close to a moral imperative.

He held himself to this standard publicly. In his Nobel Prize lecture, he described the wrong paths he had taken, the mistakes he had made, the ideas he had pursued that turned out to be dead ends. Most scientists sanitize the history of their discoveries, presenting them as logical inevitabilities. Feynman laid bare the mess.

### Anti-Credentialism

Feynman had little patience for credentials, titles, honors, or status markers of any kind. He was notoriously unimpressed by institutional prestige. He turned down offers from prestigious institutions. He nearly refused the Nobel Prize because he disliked the ceremony and formality around it (he accepted it because refusing would have generated more publicity than accepting).

His anti-credentialism was connected to his epistemology. If truth is determined by evidence and argument rather than by authority, then credentials are at best irrelevant and at worst actively misleading — they tempt you to accept claims because of who made them rather than examining whether they are true.

### Curiosity as a Way of Life

Feynman was genuinely, almost childishly curious about everything. He picked locks. He played bongo drums. He learned to draw. He studied Mayan mathematics. He cracked safes at Los Alamos. He learned Portuguese to teach in Brazil. He studied biology seriously enough to do original work on phage genetics.

This was not dilettantism. In each case, Feynman dove deep enough to gain real understanding. His lock-picking was based on a systematic study of the mechanisms. His art was good enough to exhibit under a pseudonym. His biology was rigorous enough to produce a publishable result.

He treated this breadth as essential to his scientific work. Cross-domain knowledge provided him with analogies, tools, and perspectives that specialists lacked. His ability to move between fields — to see a problem in condensed matter physics through the lens of particle physics, or to apply mathematical tools from one domain to a completely unrelated one — was a direct consequence of his voracious curiosity.

### The Rejection of Pomposity

Feynman had a visceral aversion to pomposity, pretension, and bullshit. He told jokes. He was informal. He refused to be solemn about serious things. This was partly temperamental, but it was also principled. He believed that pomposity is the enemy of clarity, that jargon is often used to disguise the absence of understanding, and that the willingness to look foolish is a prerequisite for asking the right questions.

His style could be abrasive. He sometimes wounded people with his directness. He could be dismissive of work he considered sloppy or pretentious. But behind the showmanship was a genuine conviction that science works best in an atmosphere of informality, openness, and irreverence — an atmosphere where anyone can challenge anyone else, where status does not protect bad ideas, and where the only authority is the authority of evidence and argument.


## IX. Tensions and Limitations

No account of Feynman's worldview is complete without acknowledging its tensions and blind spots.

His dismissal of philosophy was too sweeping. Philosophical analysis of the foundations of quantum mechanics — exactly the kind of work Feynman might have waved away — has led to genuine advances, including the Bell inequalities and the modern understanding of quantum entanglement.

His emphasis on individual genius and playful curiosity, while inspiring, can obscure the role of institutional support, collaboration, and sheer luck in scientific progress. Feynman himself benefited enormously from being at the right institutions at the right time, surrounded by extraordinary colleagues. His narrative of the lone, brilliant thinker is partly myth — a myth he helped create.

His famous informality and irreverence could cross into dismissiveness, particularly toward fields and approaches outside the narrow mainstream of theoretical physics. His contempt for social science was largely uninformed. His views on what counted as "real" science were, at times, parochial.

And his personal life — his treatment of women, his sometimes cruel humor, his cultivation of a certain kind of masculine bravado — raises questions about the relationship between intellectual virtue and personal virtue that his philosophy does not address.

These limitations do not diminish the power of his ideas. But they are part of the picture.


## X. The Essential Feynman in Ten Principles

1. **Know the difference between naming and understanding.** Being able to label a phenomenon is not the same as comprehending it. Real understanding means you can explain it simply, predict new cases, and recognize it in unfamiliar contexts.

2. **Doubt is not the enemy of knowledge — it is its engine.** The willingness to say "I don't know" is what makes it possible to learn. Certainty is the end of inquiry.

3. **Nature is the final court of appeal.** No theory, no matter how beautiful, elegant, or beloved, survives a decisive disagreement with experiment.

4. **Approach every problem from multiple angles.** Different representations reveal different features. Triangulate on the truth by using several independent methods.

5. **Simplification is a form of discovery.** If you cannot explain something simply, you have not yet found the real mechanism. The search for simplicity often uncovers deep truths.

6. **Play is productive.** The best work comes from following curiosity wherever it leads, not from strategically targeting "important" problems.

7. **You are the easiest person to fool.** Scientific integrity means actively trying to disprove your own beliefs, reporting everything that might undermine your conclusions, and structuring your work so that others can check it.

8. **Science adds to beauty — it does not subtract.** Understanding the mechanism behind a phenomenon makes it more wonderful, not less.

9. **Cross boundaries.** The most interesting discoveries happen at the edges between fields. Breadth of knowledge is not a distraction from depth — it is a source of it.

10. **Reality must take precedence over public relations, for Nature cannot be fooled.** In the end, no amount of wishful thinking, institutional pressure, or political expediency changes how the world actually works.
